# Chapter 1: Year One

The gas engine coughed, sputtered, then roared back to life. Dean Jacobs pumped the accelerator of the '89 Ford F-250, the manual transmission still in neutral as he coaxed the straight-six awake. The truck was a beast—repainted in multicam, sitting six inches higher on a lift kit, with manual lockers at the wheel hubs for when the terrain got serious. Surface rust showed on the frame and hitch from sitting through deployments, but Dean treated it with oil after every return, and always kept it under a cover when he was overseas. The engine ran like the day it rolled off the assembly line. Push-start capable if needed. Some things just refused to die.

He knew the feeling.

The two-track road quickly narrowed to a single wide game trail, winding through lodgepole pines and over granite outcroppings that scraped the undercarriage. Dean didn't care. The lawyer he'd hired—handling everything remotely—had said the property was "remote and undeveloped." What he'd meant was "good luck getting there." Dean had never actually been to Missoula to meet him face-to-face. Everything had been done over the phone and through the mail, the way he preferred it.

Perfect.

In the truck bed, covered by an oiled canvas tarp and lashed down with paracord, sat everything Dean owned that mattered: A canvas wall tent. Sleeping bag rated to minus twenty. Cast iron cookware. Hand tools—axes, saws, sledgehammer, wedges. A portable anvil. A leather tool roll filled with files and chisels. Fifty pounds of nails in various sizes. Coleman lanterns and fuel. Water jugs. MREs and bulk rice. Salt. A medical kit that could handle field surgery. His rifle—a Remington 700 in .308 with a Leupold scope and five hundred rounds of ammunition. And a trusty military-style 1911 in a worn leather holster.

Twenty years in the Army, and this is what he had to show for it. No wife. No kids. No mortgage in the suburbs. Just tools and the skills to use them.

Also perfect.

The truck lurched over another rock, and Dean gripped the wheel tighter. His DD-214 sat in the glove box, his discharge papers still crisp and new. Three weeks out of the service. Three weeks since his last conversation with the unit shrink, who'd suggested—politely, professionally—that maybe Dean wasn't ready for civilian life. That maybe he needed more time to "process" before making "major life decisions."

Dean's response had been equally polite: he'd signed the papers declining further counseling and walked out.

He'd processed enough. The screaming in his head hadn't stopped with therapy sessions. The faces of the little girls who'd never grow up didn't fade in air-conditioned VA offices. The smell of burning hospitals didn't wash away with prescription pills.

So he'd taken his retirement pay, his VA disability check, and bought the most remote piece of land he could find. One hundred sixty acres of Montana wilderness, so far from civilization that the real estate listing hadn't bothered with photos. The price had been almost insulting—twenty thousand dollars for land nobody wanted.

Dean wanted it.

The trees opened up ahead, and he eased the truck into what passed for a clearing. The property. His property. He positioned the truck on a slight incline—nose downhill, always—before killing the engine. A dead battery meant a push-start with the manual transmission. Habit from years of operating in places where help wasn't coming. He sat for a moment, taking it in.

A river—more of a creek, really—cut through the northern edge, rushing over rocks with that constant white noise that would either drive a man crazy or keep him sane. Dean was betting on the latter. The land rose gently to the south, covered in pine and Douglas fir, with granite ridges jutting up like broken teeth. To the east, a small valley spread out, maybe five acres of relatively flat ground covered in grass and wildflowers.

No roads. No power lines. No neighbors.

No complications.

Dean climbed out of the truck, his boots hitting soil that was his for the first time in his life. The air smelled like pine sap and clean water. A hawk circled overhead, riding thermals Dean couldn't feel. The sun was already starting its descent—he'd burned most of the day just getting here.

Work to do.

He dropped the tailgate and started unloading. The wall tent went up first—a twelve by fourteen canvas shelter he'd bought military surplus. The frame went together with the muscle memory of a hundred field deployments. Stake it down. Pull it tight. Make it home.

By the time he finished, the sun had dropped below the western ridge. Dean built a fire ring from river rocks, gathered deadfall, and got a fire going. The Coleman stove would save fuel, but tonight he wanted to feel the work in his hands. Split the kindling. Build the structure. Coax flame from spark.

This made him realize he needed to pick up a wood burning stove when he next went to town, whenever that might be.

He set the percolator on a flat rock at the edge of the fire, water and grounds inside, and waited for it to boil. The coffee came out thick enough to strip paint, exactly how he liked it. The taste brought back memories of a hundred camps most of them in hostile contries in the middle east.

Dean sat on a log he'd dragged over, rifle within reach, and stared into the fire. The flames danced, casting shadows that moved like the ghosts he'd left behind. But here, in this silence, they seemed quieter. More distant.

Maybe that would be enough.

His first night on his land. His first night as a civilian. His first night where the only person he was responsible for was himself.

The weight of it—or the absence of weight—made his chest tight. This made him think of the safety of his body armor and brothers watching his back. He didn't have them anymore. Just nature keeping humanity away, the distance providing this comfort now.

"One day at a time," he said to the fire. His voice sounded strange in the wilderness, unaccustomed to being the only human sound.

The plan was simple. Survive the first winter. Build shelter. Learn the land. In the spring, start building something permanent.

Dean drained the last of his coffee and banked the fire. Inside the tent, he laid out his sleeping bag on a foam pad and set the rifle next to his head. Old habits. Outside, the creek rushed on, and somewhere in the distance, an owl called.

He closed his eyes, and for the first time in years, the screaming in his head was quieter than the water.

---

The days blurred together in a rhythm of work that Dean welcomed. Wake at dawn. Build up the fire. Coffee. Then work until dark.

He spent the first week just exploring, walking every foot of his property with a compass and notebook. Mapping the terrain. Identifying resources. Thinking like the Green Beret he'd been trained to be—what could he use, what could kill him, what could he defend.

The creek was the lifeblood. Fast-moving, spring-fed, cold enough to hurt his teeth when he drank from it. Trout, too—he could see them darting in the shallows. Protein on demand.

The timber was lodgepole pine mostly, straight and tall, with some Douglas fir mixed in. Good for building. The red dirt along the creek bed caught his attention—iron oxide, hematite maybe. He'd need to research that.

By the second week, Dean had a routine. Mornings: hunt. He'd already taken a mule deer with the .308, a clean shot at two hundred yards. The meat hung in a tree, wrapped in game bags, cooling in the mountain air. Afternoons: gather wood, primarily felling trees and stripping the bark to let them dry. He'd need cords of it for winter, and winter in Montana didn't ask permission.

The cold came on fast in October. Dean woke one morning to frost on the tent and ice in his water bucket. Time to upgrade.

He'd been thinking about this since day one. A log cabin would take too long—he'd never finish before the snow came. But the Mongols had solved this problem a thousand years ago. A yurt. Quick to build, warm, portable if needed.

Except he wasn't going to buy one. He was going to make one.

Dean started with the deer hide. He'd scraped it, worked it with a mixture of the deer's brains and wood ash—tanning the old way, the hard way. The process took days, but when he was done, he had leather. Crude, maybe, but waterproof and strong.

One deer hide wouldn't be enough. He needed more.

The .308 spoke again three days later. Another deer. Then an elk—Dean tracked it for six hours through the timber, finally finding it bedded down in a draw. The shot was surgical. The animal died instantly. Respect the kill. Use everything.

Packing an elk out of the backcountry was a different beast than a deer. The animal weighed half a ton, and Dean had to butcher it on site, working methodically to field dress and quarter it. The meat, hide, and organs were separated into manageable loads, each piece wrapped and secured. What took two hours to kill took four hours to process.

Then came the haul. Dean fashioned a travois from deadfall and rawhide, loading the heaviest quarters onto it. The rest he distributed into his pack and a makeshift sling across his shoulders. The six-hour hike out became twelve hours coming back, fighting through brush and over deadfall, the weight grinding into his shoulders with every step. Twice he had to stop and move supplies around, redistributing the load. His hands bled from the hide wrappings. His legs trembled by the time he broke through to the clearing.

But he made it. And when he finally had the meat hung and cooling in the trees, he sat heavily on a stump and laughed—a ragged, exhausted sound. This was what it meant to live off the land. Not the hunt. The work after.

More hides. More leather. The work consumed him—scraping, stretching, treating. His hands blistered, then callused, then stopped feeling pain altogether.

By late October, he had enough hides to start building. Dean cut lodgepole pines for the frame—young trees, maybe six inches in diameter, straight as rifle barrels. He built the lattice walls the way he'd seen in books, lashing the poles together with rawhide strips. The roof frame went up next, a gentle dome that would shed snow.

Then came the hides. Dean laced them together with more rawhide, overlapping them like shingles. The leather was thick, heavy, already stiffening in the cold. He worked until his fingers were too numb to tie knots, then built up the fire and started again.

The yurt went up in a clearing he'd chosen for drainage and wind protection. Sixteen feet across. Room enough to stand. A smoke hole at the peak, and a door frame he'd built from scrap lumber.

It wasn't beautiful. But it was shelter.

Dean moved his gear inside, built a fire pit in the center with rocks from the creek, and cut a hole in the roof for the smoke to escape. The first fire he lit, smoke filled the interior until he got the draft right. But once it worked, it worked.

The hides trapped the heat. The frame creaked in the wind but held. Dean lay on his sleeping bag that first night, watching smoke curl up through the roof hole to the stars beyond, and felt something close to satisfaction.

He'd built this. With his hands. With animals he'd killed and trees he'd cut. No hardware store. No contractors. Just skill and stubbornness.

November brought the first real snow. Dean woke to six inches of white covering everything, muffling the world. The yurt held. The heat from the fire kept the interior warm enough that his breath didn't fog. He was going to make it.

But he needed more than shelter to survive. He needed metal. Tools. A way to work iron.

Dean started gathering materials for a forge. He'd watched YouTube videos back when he had internet, studied blacksmithing manuals until he could recite them. Theory was one thing. Practice would be different.

He built the forge from river rocks and Montana clay, shaping a bowl for the coal with a hole at the bottom for air. The bellows were harder—he used leather from the hides, wood for the frame, and a ridiculous amount of trial and error. The first version didn't work. Neither did the second. The third version moved air, barely, but enough.

Coal was the challenge. Dean didn't have coal. But he had wood, and wood could become charcoal.

He dug a pit six feet across and three feet deep, lined it with rocks, and filled it with hardwood—what little he could find. Mostly pine, which wasn't ideal, but it would have to do. He lit it, let it burn hot, then covered it with dirt and sod to starve the oxygen.

The process took two days. When Dean finally uncovered the pit, he had charcoal. Crude, inconsistent, but it would burn hot enough to work metal.

If he had metal.

The red dirt called to him. Hematite, iron oxide, the raw material for iron if he could get it hot enough. Dean gathered buckets of it from the creek bed, grinding it into powder with rocks. He built a crucible from Montana clay, let it dry for days, then placed it in the forge.

The first attempt failed. The crucible cracked. The second attempt, he got the clay mixture better, but the heat wasn't enough. The third attempt, he modified the bellows, added more charcoal, and pumped until his arms screamed.

The crucible glowed white-hot. Dean kept pumping. Inside, the iron oxide was reducing, separating, becoming something new.

When he finally pulled it out and let it cool, then cracked it open, there was a small blob of iron at the bottom. Maybe two ounces. Impure, slag-filled, but it was iron.

Dean held it in his hand, this metal he'd pulled from the earth with heat and muscle, and laughed. The sound echoed off the trees, startling a raven from its perch.

He could make iron. He could work metal. He could build anything.

The winter closed in hard after that. Snow piled up to his knees, then his waist. Dean spent his days feeding the fire, processing hides, making charcoal, and practicing smithing. His first attempts at forging were disasters—bent nails, cracked tools, metal that shattered when he tried to temper it.

But he was getting better. Each attempt taught him something. The way the metal moved when it was the right temperature. The sound of the hammer on the anvil when the strike was true. The feel of it in his hands when the fold was perfect.

He made nails. Crude ones, but they held. He made a knife blade from a piece of iron he'd smelted, grinding it sharp on river rocks, fitting it with a handle from antler. The edge held. It cut.

Dean also spent the winter cutting timber. He'd need logs for a real house, and winter was the best time—the sap was down, the wood would dry better. He felled pines with an axe and bow saw, limbing them, cutting them to length. Each tree took hours. He didn't care. The work kept him warm. The work kept him sane.

By February, he had a dozen logs cut and stacked. By March, two dozen. By April, when the snow finally started to melt, he had fifty logs, ranging from eight to sixteen feet, all debarked and drying.

Spring came slowly, then all at once. The creek swelled with runoff. The valley turned green. Flowers Dean couldn't name burst from the ground in riots of color.

He'd survived his first winter. He'd built shelter, made fire, hunted food, and created metal from dirt. His hands were scarred and calloused. His beard was long and wild. His body was leaner, harder, stripped of anything that wasn't necessary.

Dean stood outside the yurt one morning in late April, watching the sun rise over the eastern ridge, and took inventory. He had shelter. He had food. He had tools. He had iron.

Next year, he'd build a house. A real one. Stone foundation, log walls, a roof that would last decades.

But this year—this first year—he'd learned something more important. He'd learned that he could survive alone. That he could build a life with nothing but his hands and his will.

The screaming in his head hadn't stopped completely. But out here, with the work and the silence and the cold clean air, it was bearable.

Dean walked to the creek, knelt down, and splashed water on his face. The shock of it—ice-melt cold—brought him fully into the moment. This moment. This place. This life he was building one hammer strike, one log, one day at a time.

---

By late May, Dean realized he needed supplies that he couldn't make or salvage. Real tools. Seeds. Materials for the projects he was planning. It was time to go to town.

Missoula was a hundred miles away, the closest city of any size. The drive took nearly four hours on rough roads. Dean hadn't been around people since his discharge. The thought made his jaw tight.

But he went.

He cleaned up as best he could in the creek—scrubbed the ash and dirt from his beard, wore clothes that weren't covered in soot and blood. He still looked like a wild man when he caught his reflection in the truck's side mirror. Long hair, scarred hands, eyes that had learned to see in harsh light and harder truths.

Missoula hit him like a shock. The noise. The people. The buildings crowded together like they had nowhere else to go. He'd lived a hundred miles from civilization and a world away. The town smelled like exhaust and asphalt and something he'd almost forgotten existed—normal human life.

Dean moved through the hardware store like a ghost, gathering what he needed. More nails. Hinges. A wood stove—the one he'd decided on months ago. Tarps. Rope. Pressure-treated lumber for building. The clerk didn't ask questions, just rang it up. Cash transaction. No names. No history.

At the feed store, he bought seeds—vegetable seeds, grain seeds, herb seeds. A young girl rang him up, eyeing his appearance with barely concealed alarm.

"You starting a farm?" she asked, just trying to make conversation.

"Something like that," Dean said. He didn't elaborate.

At the pharmacy, he bought basic medical supplies—bandages, antibiotics, pain relievers. More of what he already had, but better. Industrial quantities.

He parked near the library and went inside, the cool quiet a relief. He used their computer to download PDFs—blacksmithing guides, water system designs, homestead building techniques. He printed them out, burned them to a thumb drive, spent an hour on research that would have taken him months to figure out otherwise.

The librarian, an older woman with kind eyes, watched him leave with a knowing look. Montana was full of people running from something. She'd learned not to ask questions.

By afternoon, the truck was loaded. Wood stove in the bed, secured with paracord. Seeds and supplies packed carefully. Dean sat in a diner and ate a real meal—burger and fries, nothing fancy, but it tasted like the world beyond his property. He paid cash and left no tip, left no memory.

The drive back took him through late afternoon into dusk. The highway wound north, leaving civilization behind, the trees growing denser, the landscape wilder. With each mile, his shoulders relaxed. With each turn away from town, he felt himself returning to the person he'd become.

By the time he reached the turnoff to his property, it was dark. The truck bounced over the game trail, branches scraping the sides, the headlights cutting through the forest. Home. More real than any town could be.

He parked and sat in the cab for a moment, breathing air that smelled like pine and earth and freedom. The yurt sat dark in the clearing. The creek rushed in the distance. The stars above were so bright they cast faint shadows.

Dean unloaded the wood stove first, dragging it inside the yurt. Tomorrow he'd figure out how to install it, how to run the chimney through the leather roof. Tonight, he just sat in the darkness and listened to the creek.

This—this right here—was worth every moment in that crowded, noisy, suffocating town. This was worth the struggle, the pain, the endless work.

This was his.

Dean walked to the creek, knelt down, and splashed water on his face. The shock of it—ice-melt cold—brought him fully into the moment. This moment. This place. This life he was building one hammer strike, one log, one day at a time.

"Year one," he said to the water.

The creek rushed on, indifferent to his words, eternal in its purpose.

Dean smiled, picked up his axe, and got back to work.

---

*End of Chapter 1*
# Chapter 2: Year Two

Spring melt turned the creek into a roaring monster. Dean stood on the bank, watching brown water surge past, carrying branches and debris from higher elevations. The runoff would last another month, maybe two, before settling back into the manageable flow he'd known last year.

But he wasn't waiting. He had work to do.

The yurt had served its purpose through the winter, keeping him alive through temperatures that dropped to minus thirty. But it was temporary shelter, and Dean didn't do temporary. Not anymore. This land was his, and he was going to build something that would outlast him.

A house. A real one. Stone and log, built the way structures used to be built—to last centuries.

He started with the foundation.

Dean had spent the late winter months walking the creek bed during rare warm spells, selecting stones. Not just any stones—he needed granite, hard and weather-resistant, ranging from the size of a football to the size of a hay bale. He'd marked the good ones, planning his routes, calculating how many trips it would take.

The answer was: a lot.

The foundation site was two hundred yards uphill from the creek, on a slight rise that offered good drainage and a view of the valley. Dean had already cleared the ground, removing topsoil down to bedrock in places, leveling the area with a shovel and a will that refused to quit.

Twelve feet by sixteen feet. That's all he needed. Room for living, sleeping, and working. Anything bigger was just ego.

He built a stone sled from logs and rope—a something he could drag behind himself. Load it with rocks from the creek, haul it uphill, unload, repeat. Each trip took an hour. He made six trips a day.

His legs burned. His back screamed. His shoulders felt like they were tearing apart.

Dean didn't care. This was simple math: more trips meant more stones meant more foundation meant permanent shelter. So he made more trips.

By late April, he had enough stones to start building. The foundation design was simple—stone pillars at each corner and at intervals along the walls, with space between them to allow for airflow beneath the floor. It would keep the logs off the ground, prevent rot, and give him storage space for tools and supplies.

He mixed mortar from creek sand, clay from the banks, and lime he'd made by burning limestone rocks in his forge. The chemistry was crude, but it worked—the mixture hardened into something that would hold stones together.

Dean laid the first stone of the northeast corner pillar on May first. He bedded it in mortar, checked it for level with a straight edge and a water-filled glass. Level. Good. The next stone went on top, then another.

The work was meditative. Select a stone. Test the fit. Trim with a hammer and chisel if needed. Mix mortar. Lay it down. Set the stone. Check for level. Check for plumb. Adjust. Let it set.

Hours would pass, and Dean would realize he hadn't thought about anything except the stones. No screaming. No explosions. No ghosts. Just granite and mortar and the satisfaction of seeing the pillar grow taller.

By June, the foundation pillars were done. Eight of them, each standing four feet tall, each one perfectly plumb and level. Dean had checked them so many times he saw them in his sleep.

Now came the logs.

He'd spent last winter cutting them. Now he needed to mill them. A chainsaw would have made it easy, but Dean didn't have one, didn't want one. The noise would shatter the silence he'd come here to find. Besides, the old ways worked.

He built a sawpit—a rectangular trench six feet deep and ten feet long, with a platform across the top. The principle was ancient: one man above the log, one man below, working a two-man saw to rip the log into lumber.

Except Dean didn't have a second man. He had ingenuity and stubbornness.

He rigged a system using counterweights and ropes, allowing him to pull the saw down from below, then use a weighted lever to pull it back up for the next stroke. It was slow. It was exhausting. But it worked.

The first log took three days to mill into four rough planks. The second took two days. By the tenth log, Dean had it down to a day and a half. The sawdust piled up like snow, and his arms felt like rubber, but the lumber stacked up.

Planks for flooring. Beams for the frame. Boards for the roof. Each piece rough-cut and heavy, smelling of pine sap and sawdust.

July brought heat that baked the valley. Dean worked shirtless, his skin darkening to the color of old leather. Sweat ran in rivers. He drank water straight from the creek and kept working.

The log walls went up. He'd notched each log at the ends—saddle notches, cut with an axe and chisel, each one fitting into the log below like puzzle pieces. The corners locked together with a strength that would resist earthquakes.

Lift the log. Position it. Check the fit. Adjust. Spike it down with the nails he'd forged last winter. Next log. Repeat.

By mid-July, the walls were chest-high. By early August, they were up to the roof line. Dean stood inside the structure one evening, looking up at the sky through the open top, and felt something he hadn't felt in years.

Pride.

He'd built this. Every stone, every log, every nail. His hands. His back. His will.

The roof went on next. Rafters from milled lumber, shakes split from straight-grained pine, overlapped like scales. No nails on the shakes—he pegged them with wood dowels he'd carved by hand, the way roofs had been built for a thousand years.

When the last shake went on, Dean climbed down from the roof, walked to the creek, and collapsed in the shallows. The water rushed around him, cold enough to shock, and he laughed. The sound echoed off the valley walls, startling a family of deer grazing near the tree line.

"I did it," he said to the water. "I built a goddamn house."

The creek had no opinion on the matter, but Dean didn't care. He had a house. A real one. With stone pillars that would last a century and log walls that would stand up to anything Montana could throw at them.

But a house wasn't a homestead. Not yet.

---

Late August, Dean made the trip to town. The old gas-powered truck coughed to life after sitting unused for months, and he drove the game trail up to the two-track road back to civilization—or what passed for it.

Ovando was barely a town. Population seventy on a good day, consisting of a general store, a bar, a trading post, and a scattering of houses. But it had what Dean needed: a community board where people posted things for sale.

He found what he was looking for on a handwritten index card: "Livestock trailer, $800 OBO. Good condition."

Dean tracked down the seller—a rancher named Tom, weathered and taciturn, who took one look at Dean's cash and didn't ask questions. The trailer was old but solid, dual axle, big enough for what Dean had in mind.

"You starting a ranch out there?" Tom asked, hooking the trailer to Dean's truck.

"Something like that," Dean said.

"Good luck. It's hard work."

"I've noticed."

From there, Dean hit the farm supply store—the closest thing to a hardware store within a hundred miles. He bought chicken wire, fence posts, hand tools he couldn't forge himself, seeds for next year's garden, and seed potatoes for planting in the spring.

Then came the livestock.

A farmer on the edge of town had chickens for sale—Rhode Island Reds, good layers, five dollars a bird. Dean bought six hens and a rooster. They went into crates in the truck bed, complaining loudly about the indignity.

At another farm, he found goats—two does and a buck, Nigerian Dwarfs, hardy and good for milk. The farmer wanted two hundred for all three. Dean paid it without haggling.

Pigs came next. A breeding pair of American Guinea Hogs, small and efficient, perfect for a homesteader. The seller was asking four hundred. Dean negotiated down to three-fifty and loaded them into the trailer.

The truck groaned under the weight on the drive back. The animals protested. Dean ignored them, focused on the two-track road and the mental list of everything he still needed to do before winter.

Back at the homestead, he unloaded the animals into temporary pens he'd built from saplings and rope. The chickens settled in quickly, already scratching at the dirt. The goats eyed him suspiciously, then started eating everything in reach. The pigs rooted in the mud, content.

Dean stood back and surveyed his property. A house. Livestock. The beginnings of a farm.

It still didn't feel real.

---

Fall came on fast. Dean spent September and October building permanent structures for the animals. A chicken coop with proper ventilation and roosting bars, elevated off the ground to keep predators out. A goat shed, three-sided with a roof, opening to the south to maximize sun exposure. A pig pen with a wallow and a shelter they could root under.

He also planted what he could for next year—garlic cloves, which would overwinter and be ready in early summer. He mulched a garden plot with leaves and pine needles, preparing the soil for spring planting.

The work was endless. But it was good work. Useful work. Work that meant something.

By November, when the first real snow fell, Dean had a house with a woodstove he'd built from an old barrel and iron strapping. The chimney drafted perfectly, pulling smoke up and out, keeping the interior warm even when the temperature outside dropped below zero.

He had livestock that were fed and sheltered. He had firewood stacked to the roof. He had meat hanging in a smokehouse he'd built from green logs and mud.

---

But before the deep winter closed in, Dean had one more project in mind.

Self-sufficiency meant redundancy. It meant backup plans. It meant never being caught unprepared.

He spent a week in late October scouting the far corners of his property—the ridges, the ravines, the places where the forest grew thick enough to swallow a man whole. He needed a location that was hidden, elevated, memorable, and defensible. In theory. Though he hoped he'd never need that.

He found it on the north face of the eastern ridge, in a rocky outcrop where a natural cave had formed in the granite. The entrance was narrow, hidden behind a fallen pine that had taken decades to rot. Inside, the cave opened up—maybe eight feet deep, five feet wide. Just enough.

Perfect.

Dean gathered materials over two weeks. Ammunition first—he had several thousand rounds. He packed them in waterproof ammunition cans, sealed with duct tape and wrapped in oilcloth. Rifle rounds, pistol rounds, shotgun shells if he ever got a shotgun. Enough to sustain him through most scenarios.

Medical supplies came next. A duplicate of everything in his main medical kit, plus extras—antibiotics in sealed containers, trauma dressings, sutures, tourniquets, morphine tablets he'd kept from his Army days. A manual on wilderness trauma care. Everything he'd need to treat serious injuries if he couldn't reach civilization.

Food was the third layer. Cases of MREs, canned goods, salt, sugar, coffee, dried beans. Enough for three months if everything else failed. Water purification tablets, a hand pump filter.

Tools came last. A complete set of hand tools duplicating what he had at the house—hammers, saws, chisels, files. A bow saw with spare blades. Paracord. Duct tape. A hand-crank flashlight. Candles. Strike-anywhere matches in sealed tins.

He packed everything in waterproof containers—army-surplus ammo boxes, plastic bins with tight-fitting lids—and carried them one load at a time to the cave. The walk took thirty minutes from the house, cutting cross-country through timber and over granite.

On the final trip, he sat inside the cave and looked at what he'd assembled. Enough to survive. Enough to start again if something happened to everything at the house. A ghost of his old life reasserting itself—the tactical mind never shut off, always planning for contingencies.

He marked the location in his notebook using a grid system he created himself, with landmarks instead of GPS coordinates. The tallest tree in the eastern valley. The twin granite peaks. The creek that ran through the ravine. Nothing that would mean anything to anyone else. Nothing that could be found unless you knew exactly what you were looking for.

Dean sat in the darkness of the cave for an hour, just thinking. This felt paranoid. It probably was. But paranoia had kept him alive for twenty years. And out here, alone, the old habits died hard.

He sealed the cave entrance as well as he could without making it obvious, arranging rocks and brush to discourage casual investigation. Then he walked back to the house through the fading light, noting landmarks, committing the route to memory.

By the time he reached his house, the sun had dropped below the western ridge. He built up the fire and sat with his notebook, documenting everything in a cipher only he could read. Location. Contents. Access route. Renewal schedule—he'd check it every year, rotate supplies, make sure nothing had degraded.

A secondary cache. An insurance policy. A hedge against the unknown.

---

Dean sat in his house one evening, the woodstove crackling, coffee heating in a pot on top, and opened a notebook. He'd been keeping notes—what worked, what didn't, what he'd do differently next year.

Year Two had been about building permanence. Year Three would be about refinement. Expanding the garden. Breeding the livestock. Building infrastructure—better water collection, maybe even a small mill for grain if he could manage it.

The screaming in his head was still there. It probably always would be. But here, in this house he'd built with his hands, surrounded by the life he was creating, it was distant. Manageable.

Dean closed the notebook, picked up the coffee, and walked to the window. Outside, snow fell in fat flakes, covering the valley in white. The animals were quiet in their shelters. The creek was already starting to freeze at the edges.

His second winter. His second year. And he was ready for it.

"Year two," he said to the snow.

The flakes fell silently, indifferent to his words, eternal in their purpose.

Dean smiled, added another log to the stove, and settled in for the long night ahead.

# Chapter 3: Year Three

Spring arrived late, reluctant, as if winter didn't want to surrender its grip on the valley. Dean didn't mind. He'd used the extra time to finish the interior of the house—shelves built into the walls, a workbench by the south window, a sleeping loft above the main room that trapped heat like a blanket.

But when the thaw finally came in late April, it came hard. The creek exploded with runoff, and Dean stood at its banks with a new project in mind.

He needed water. Not just to drink—he had that from the creek. He needed water on demand, up at the house, without having to haul buckets twice a day like some medieval peasant.

The ram pump had been on his mind since last fall. The principle was simple: use the creek's own momentum to push water uphill. No fuel, no electricity, just physics and persistence. The kind of technology that had worked for two hundred years because it was too simple to break.

Dean had sketched designs in his notebook all winter. Now it was time to build.

He started by gathering materials. PVC pipe—he'd bought a hundred feet of it on his last supply run to Missoula, along with valves and fittings. Iron for the check valves, which he'd forge himself. A steel drum for the drive pipe chamber.

The site he chose was a hundred yards downstream from the house, where the creek had a natural drop of about four feet over a short distance. Perfect for generating the pressure pulse the ram pump needed.

Dean dug a trench from the creek to the installation site, bedded the drive pipe, and assembled the check valves in his forge. The work was exacting—the valves needed to move freely but seal completely, allowing water to flow one direction but not the other.

It took three tries to get them right. The first version leaked. The second stuck. The third worked.

He installed the ram pump chamber, connected everything, and opened the valve.

Nothing happened.

Dean adjusted the waste valve. Still nothing.

He rechecked his calculations, his measurements, his valve clearances. Everything should work. In theory.

He opened the waste valve wider.

The pump coughed. Water spurted. Then, with a rhythmic thump-thump-thump, the ram pump came to life.

Water pulsed up the delivery pipe. Dean followed its path with his eyes, watching it climb the hill, defying gravity, pushed by nothing but the creek's own momentum.

At the house, he'd installed a cistern—a 250-gallon tank he'd built from cedar planks, sealed with pine pitch. The delivery pipe fed into the top. Dean watched as water began to trickle in.

Trickle became stream. Stream became flow.

The cistern started to fill.

Dean let out a breath he didn't know he'd been holding. The ram pump worked. He had running water. Sort of. Gravity-fed from the cistern, available whenever he opened a tap he'd installed in the kitchen.

"Running water," he said aloud. "Like a real person."

The creek didn't answer, but the ram pump kept thumping, steady as a heartbeat, doing its job without complaint.

---

With water handled, Dean turned his attention to power.

The micro-turbine had been another winter project in his mind. He'd sketched designs, calculated flow rates, estimated power output. The creek had enough flow and drop to generate electricity—not much, maybe enough to charge a battery bank and run LED lights, but that was enough.

Dean fabricated the turbine housing from an old steel tank he'd found half-buried near the property line—someone's trash from decades ago, now his resource. The rusted cylinder had probably been a propane tank once, but now it was just scrap metal. He cut it open, cleaned it out, and shaped it into a housing for the turbine wheel.

The wheel itself was the tricky part. He needed something that would spin efficiently in the creek's flow, capturing as much energy as possible. Dean carved the blades from hardwood, shaped like airplane propellers, and mounted them on a steel shaft he'd forged and machined by hand with files.

The generator came from Missoula—a DC motor from a treadmill someone was throwing away. Dean got it for twenty bucks. Run a motor backward, and it becomes a generator. Basic physics.

He installed the turbine in the creek, anchored to rocks and logs, positioned where the water flowed fastest. Connected the generator. Ran wire up to the house.

The first test, the turbine spun but generated almost nothing. The blades weren't pitched right. Dean pulled it out, adjusted the angle, tested again.

Better, but not enough.

Three iterations later, he had it dialed in. The turbine spun smooth and fast, the generator humming, producing twelve volts DC. Dean connected it to a car battery he'd bought used, creating the simplest possible battery bank.

That night, for the first time in three years, Dean had electric light in his house. An LED bulb, powered by the creek, casting cool white light across the log walls.

He sat at his workbench, staring at the bulb, and laughed. It was such a small thing. Such a simple thing. But it meant something. It meant he was building more than just shelter. He was building civilization. His civilization.

---

But a single car battery had limitations. Dean knew this from his research, from the PDFs he'd downloaded and studied during long winter nights. A twelve-volt lead-acid battery could store charge, but it couldn't handle the demands of a growing system. If he wanted to add more lights, power tools, eventually maybe a refrigeration unit for the milk from the goats, he needed capacity.

In early June, on another supply run to Missoula, Dean went hunting for batteries.

The farm supply store had what he needed: four deep-cycle marine batteries, the kind used for RVs and boats. Heavy bastards—each one weighed over fifty pounds. He bought them without haggling at the price, loaded them into the truck with the same methodical care he'd use for ammunition.

The salesman eyed him while ringing them up. "Running a big rig out there?"

"Building a system," Dean said. Nothing more.

He also bought something he'd been eyeing for months: an MPPT charge controller. The saleswoman showed him the specs—Maximum Power Point Tracking, designed for solar or small wind installations, with safety features to prevent overcharging and damage to the battery bank. The display would show voltage, current, battery status. Everything he'd need to manage the system properly.

"You got solar panels too?" she asked.

"Turbine," Dean said. "Small hydro."

She nodded like this was a completely normal thing, which in rural Montana, maybe it was.

Back at the homestead, Dean spent an afternoon planning the battery setup. He couldn't just connect four new batteries in series or parallel without understanding the math. Voltage and amperage. Series multiplied voltage. Parallel multiplied capacity. He wanted to keep twelve volts—compatible with everything he had—but dramatically increase the amp-hour rating.

He built a battery box from scrap lumber, insulated it with straw and old blankets to help maintain temperature. The four new batteries went in, configured in a parallel arrangement with the original car battery. The connections had to be perfect—heavy gauge copper wire, properly crimped terminals, fuses on each battery to prevent catastrophic damage if something shorted.

Then came the charge controller installation.

Dean mounted it on the workshop wall, near the battery box. The wiring was meticulous: positive from the turbine generator, negative from the generator, positive to battery, negative from battery, all routed through the controller. The device had a small LCD display that flickered to life as soon as the first wires connected.

He double-checked everything. Triple-checked it. A mistake here could start a fire, destroy equipment, or worse.

When he was satisfied, Dean fired up the system.

The turbine was still spinning—it never really stopped, just adjusted with the creek's flow. As he opened the valve on the delivery pipe, water pressure increased. The turbine spun faster. The generator's hum deepened.

The charge controller's LCD display flickered, then came alive.

Input voltage: 14.2V. Current flowing in: forty-eight amps from the turbine, feeding into the battery bank. The display cycled through screens—showing him the amperage, the charging rate, the battery bank voltage.

Sixteen point two volts. The system was working exactly as it should.

Dean watched the battery status indicator climb: 20%, 25%, 30%. The four new batteries, combined with the original car battery, were accepting power, storing it, creating a reservoir of energy that would let him do things he couldn't do before.

"This was nothing like that first car battery," he said aloud. "This is a system."

He sat in his workshop for hours, just watching the numbers on the display. The rain came that afternoon, increasing the creek's flow, and the turbine spun faster. The charge controller adjusted automatically, regulating the input to prevent overcharging, maintaining the battery bank at optimal levels.

By evening, the display showed 92% charge. The system had capacity to spare. Dean could run lights all night, power tools during the day, and the turbine would keep feeding energy back into the batteries.

He'd done it. He'd built a power system. A real one.

Dean walked outside and stood by the creek, listening to the ram pump's rhythmic thump and watching the turbine spin in the fading light. Water and electricity. The two forces that moved civilization. And he'd harnessed both of them, without grid power, without fuel, without dependence on anything but the mountain and his own skill.

He thought about building, about creating infrastructure that would last. About being self-sufficient. About never being at anyone else's mercy again.

The turbine spun on, feeding the system, and Dean smiled.

The garden that year was ambitious. Dean had learned from last year's mistakes—where the frost settled, which areas dried out too fast, what the deer would eat no matter how he tried to protect it.

He expanded the plot to a quarter acre, amending the soil with manure from the goats and pigs, compost from kitchen scraps and bedding. He planted in blocks: potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips. Cabbage and kale for storage. Beans and peas for protein. Squash and pumpkins because they grew like weeds.

He also planted an orchard—thirty apple tree saplings he'd bought from a nursery in Missoula. They wouldn't bear fruit for years, but Dean was thinking long-term now. Five years, ten years, twenty years down the line, he'd have apples.

The chickens had multiplied. The hens had gone broody in spring, hatching out clutches of chicks that peered and scratched their way into the world. Dean now had twenty birds, all descended from the original seven. Eggs for days.

The goats had kidded—two babies from each doe, tiny and bouncing with ridiculous energy. Dean kept one female from each line to grow the herd, sold the others to a farmer in Ovando for cash he didn't really need but saved anyway.

The pigs had pigged, producing a litter of six. Dean kept two females, sold the rest. By fall, he'd have breeding stock enough to never need to buy pork again.

His homestead was becoming self-sustaining. Not just surviving, but thriving.

---

Summer that year was a blur of work. Dean's days started before dawn and ended after dark. Tending the garden. Feeding livestock. Repairing the endless small things that broke. Building new structures—a springhouse for cold storage, a workshop separate from the house, a barn for hay storage.

He cut hay from the meadow with a scythe, working in the cool of the morning, moving through the grass in a rhythm older than civilization. The blade whispered through the stems, and the hay fell in neat rows. He let it dry in the sun, then stacked it in the barn—feed for the goats and bedding for all the animals through winter.

He hunted, too. Deer for the freezer—wait, he didn't have a freezer. For the smokehouse, then. Elk when he could find them. Grouse and rabbits for variety. The .308 spoke less often now; he'd learned where the animals moved, when to wait, how to make every shot count.

Dean also spent time improving his metalworking. The forge had been rebuilt twice, each iteration better than the last. He could now produce decent quality iron, and he'd started experimenting with steel—adding carbon from charcoal, folding the metal to distribute it evenly.

He made tools he couldn't buy: specialized chisels, draw knives, a froe for splitting shakes. Each one was rough compared to store-bought, but each one was his.

There was satisfaction in that. In knowing that if the world ended tomorrow—if the stores closed, if civilization collapsed, if everything went to hell—he'd be fine. He could make what he needed. Grow what he needed. Build what he needed.

Self-sufficiency wasn't just a goal anymore. It was reality.

In July, Dean made another trip to Missoula. Not for supplies this time, but for information.

The library had internet access, and Dean spent an afternoon researching: better garden techniques, livestock breeding, food preservation, timber framing, water systems. He downloaded PDFs to a thumb drive, printed articles, took notes in his notebook.

While he was there, an idea struck him. He had the power now—the turbine was generating solid DC current, and he'd rigged up a decent battery bank. What if he could actually have reliable internet? Not for entertainment, but for knowledge. For operational awareness. For emergencies.

He asked the librarian about satellite internet. Starlink, she said, was the most practical option for remote areas like his. No infrastructure required. Just a dish and an account.

"Can I order it here?" he asked.

She showed him the website. Dean sat at one of the public computers and created an account, ordered a Starlink kit using a credit card he barely used. Shipping address: general delivery, Ovando Post Office. He wasn't about to have packages delivered to his property—that would require a mailbox on the road, and mailboxes meant visibility.

Two weeks, the website said. He could wait.

Knowledge was a tool, same as a hammer. The more you had, the more you could do.

At the feed store, he bought grain—wheat, oats, and barley seed. Next year, he'd try growing his own grain. The meadow had enough space, and the soil seemed right. If he could grow grain, he could have flour. If he had flour, he had bread.

Complete self-sufficiency. That was the goal.

On the way out of town, Dean stopped at a used equipment lot and bought a hand-crank grain mill for fifty bucks. The owner looked at him like he was crazy.

"You know they make electric ones, right?" the guy said.

"I know," Dean replied. "But electric ones stop working when the power goes out."

The guy shrugged. "Your money."

Dean loaded the mill into the truck and drove home, already planning where he'd install it.

But before leaving town, Dean made one more stop.

The amateur radio shop was tucked between a laundromat and a closed insurance office, easy to miss if you weren't looking. Inside, shelves were packed with radios, antennas, cables, power supplies. The guy behind the counter—bearded, weathered, with the look of someone who'd spent decades talking to strangers over the airwaves—glanced up.

"Help you?" he asked.

"I'm looking for a radio," Dean said. "Something that can reach far. Something that doesn't need grid power."

The man's eyes brightened. He'd seen Dean's type before—prepared, serious, asking the right questions. "What kind of range you need?"

"Hundred miles, minimum. Five hundred if conditions are right."

"You a prepper?"

"Self-sufficient," Dean corrected.

The man nodded like that was close enough. He pulled a radio from the shelf—a PRC-117, military grade, ruggedized. "This is what you want. Originally a military rig, now available for civilian use. Two hundred watts, can hit a hundred miles easy under normal conditions. Up to five hundred with a good antenna in right conditions. Needs about thirteen volts DC to run—solar panel, battery bank, anything like that."

Dean examined the radio. It was heavy, solid, the kind of equipment that was built to work in harsh conditions. Just like everything else he was building.

"How much?"

"Eight hundred. But I've got a refurbished one, almost new condition, for five."

Dean bought the refurbished rig along with mounting hardware, a quality antenna, coax cable, and connectors. He also picked up a frequency guide and a book on amateur radio operation. The total came to nearly seven hundred dollars.

"You going to get licensed?" the shop owner asked while ringing it up.

"No," Dean said simply. "Just need to listen."

The man raised an eyebrow but didn't push. "Fair enough. That rig will work on receive without a license. Though I'll say, amateur radio community is pretty good. A lot of good people out there."

Dean paid in cash, loaded the radio equipment into the truck bed, and left without further comment. Listening was all he needed—operational awareness, emergency frequencies, knowing what was happening in the world without being part of it.

Back at the homestead, Dean installed the radio in the workshop, running the antenna wire up through the roof to a length of copper tubing he mounted on the ridge. He connected it to a dedicated circuit from the battery bank, with a fused disconnect so he could isolate it if needed. The radio came to life with a satisfying hum, the display flickering on, showing signal strength across multiple frequencies.

That first night, Dean sat in the workshop and listened to the frequencies. Emergency channels from the county sheriff. State police traffic. Amateur operators checking in on the two-meter band. A weather service broadcast from Missoula. All of it coming from the far corners of the valley and beyond, delivered through his antenna and receiver.

It was a lifeline. Not to civilization—he didn't want that. But to awareness. To knowing that if something happened, if there was an emergency, he'd have warning. He wouldn't be blindsided by events in a world he'd chosen to leave.

Dean adjusted the frequency dial, listening to voices of strangers living lives he no longer understood, and found something close to peace in the knowledge that he could hear them. That he had options. That he wasn't completely cut off.

The radio would become his constant companion, always on, always listening, a soft hum in the background of his homestead life.

Three weeks later, Dean made another trip to Ovando. The postmaster—a weathered woman named Dorothy who'd stopped asking questions years ago—had a box for him. Heavy. Unmarked except for his name and general delivery.

"Got everything you need?" she asked, scanning the label.

"Getting there," Dean said, paying cash and carrying the box back to his truck.

At home, he opened the kit: a sleek satellite dish, cables, a modem unit, installation hardware. He mounted the dish on the south-facing roof of his workshop, ran the cables inside, and connected everything to his battery bank through a charge controller.

The thing worked. One hour after installation, Dean was connected. Forty megabits down, twelve up. Better than he'd expected at his latitude and location.

He sat in his workshop for an hour just browsing the internet—news sites, engineering forums, weather reports. All the information in the world, beamed down from orbit, available whenever he needed it.

Power and knowledge. The foundation of everything.

But water—clean water—was the foundation of survival.

The cistern had been feeding the house for months now, gravity-fed from the ram pump's delivery line. It worked fine for washing. But Dean had been thinking about drinking water. The creek was clean—spring-fed, cold, fast-moving. But clean wasn't the same as safe. Parasites, bacteria, things he couldn't see but could definitely kill him.

He'd read about sand filters in one of the engineering books he'd downloaded. Simple. Elegant. The kind of solution that had worked for centuries before anyone invented plastic cartridges and pressure tanks.

Dean spent a weekend preparing. First, the sand. He walked down to the creek at a point where the water slowed and settled, where the finest silt collected in eddies. He filled buckets with clean sand, carrying them back to the house, calculating volumes and depths.

In the workshop, he set up a test batch. A glass jar, layered with materials in the order he'd read about: gravel at the bottom, then larger sand, then finer sand, then the finest sand at the top. He poured untreated creek water into the jar and watched it filter through.

It worked. The water that came out the bottom was noticeably clearer. Not perfect—he could still see the faint cloudiness that meant particles were there—but better.

But the sand itself had to be clean. That meant boiling.

Dean spent three days boiling sand. Buckets of it, heated in large pots on the woodstove, stirred constantly, allowed to cool and dry. It was tedious, pointless-feeling work, but every grain of sand had to be purified. If he was going to drink this water, depend on it, trust his life to it, then he had to do it right.

He built the filter in stages. First, a wooden box—simple construction, mortise and tenon joints, held together with hide glue. About three feet deep, two feet square. Sealed on the bottom with careful fitting and pitch sealant so water wouldn't leak through the grain in the wood.

Then came the layering. Gravel at the bottom—washed and dried, the biggest pieces he could find, fist-sized. Above that, an inch of larger gravel, maybe walnut-sized. Then larger sand, the kind that slipped through your fingers in drifts. Then medium sand. Then the fine sand, boiled and dried, packed carefully into the upper eight inches.

At the very top, a cloth layer held in place with a simple frame. That was the catch point for the last of the sediment, the final barrier before water entered the system.

The water inlet was a simple bamboo pipe—he'd salvaged a piece from an old decorative screen in Missoula—inserted through the side at the top, allowing him to slowly pour untreated water in. The outlet was another bamboo tube near the bottom, with a simple valve he'd fashioned from a piece of wood and a leather seal.

For the first test, Dean poured untreated creek water—deliberately taken from a murky section, full of sediment—into the inlet. He waited. The water filtered through the layers, slower than he'd expected, taking an hour to drip through the first batch.

But what came out was clear. Genuinely clear. He could hold it up to the light and see straight through it.

Dean filled a glass, let it settle overnight to be sure nothing was floating in it, then drank. It tasted like water. Clean water. The kind of water that wouldn't kill him.

He sat on the porch that evening, satisfied in a way that purchasing anything from a store could never achieve. This filter was his design, built with his hands, made from materials on his property or scavenged from civilization. It would clean water as long as he maintained it, replaced the sand when necessary, kept the cloth layer fresh.

Self-sufficiency meant not just producing food and energy, but producing safety. Producing health. Producing the foundational things that kept a person alive.

The sand filter would connect to the cistern system, providing one more layer of security. Fresh water, filtered and tested, available whenever he needed it. Not dependent on anything but the creek and his own knowledge.

Dean refilled his glass and raised it to the setting sun. "Clean water," he said. "One more thing conquered."

The creek sang its eternal song, and Dean smiled.

By November, when the first snow fell, Dean was ready. The animals were fat and healthy. The barn was full of hay. The root cellar was packed. The woodshed was stacked to the rafters.

He sat in his house one evening, the woodstove crackling, a cup of tea steaming in his hands—not coffee, but tea he'd made from foraged pine needles and mint he'd grown in the garden.

Three years. Three years since he'd bought this land. Three years since he'd walked away from everything.

Dean looked around the house. Sturdy log walls. A roof that didn't leak. Shelves filled with food he'd grown. Tools he'd made. A life he'd built from nothing.

The screaming in his head was still there. It probably always would be. But it was quieter now. Distant. Like a radio station fading in and out, sometimes loud, sometimes barely audible.

Out here, in the silence and the work and the clean cold air, Dean had found something he didn't know he was looking for.

Peace. Maybe not complete. Maybe not permanent. But real.

He'd built a homestead. Not just a place to survive, but a place to live.

Dean raised the cup of tea in a silent toast to the empty room. "Year three," he said.

Outside, snow fell silently, blanketing the valley in white. The animals slept in their shelters. The creek sang its endless song. And Dean Jacobs, former Green Beret, current homesteader, sat in the house he'd built and allowed himself to feel something close to contentment.

The work would continue tomorrow. It always did. But tonight, he could rest.

He'd earned it.

# Chapter 4: The Last Good Year

The hammer rang against the anvil in a rhythm Dean Jacobs knew better than his own heartbeat. Strike. Turn. Strike. The iron glowed orange-white in the dim light of the forge, bleeding heat into the cool Montana morning. He'd been at this for an hour, shaping a new hinge for the chicken coop door—the old one had finally given up after three years of mountain weather.

Strike. Turn. Strike.

The metal bent to his will, folding over itself as he worked the chisel. Perfect. One more heat and he'd have it. Dean thrust the piece back into the coals, pumping the bellows with his free hand. The charcoal—made from his own lumber, burned in a pit he'd dug himself—flared bright. Everything here, he'd built with his own hands. The forge. The bellows. Even the iron came from the red dirt of his own land, smelted in a crucible he'd shaped from Montana clay.

Self-sufficiency wasn't a hobby. It was a religion.

The sun crept over the eastern ridge, painting the valley in shades of amber. Dean paused, wiping sweat from his forehead despite the September chill. His homestead spread before him—a hundred and sixty acres of untouched wilderness, about as far from civilization as you could get in the lower forty-eight. The log home he'd built sat nestled against a pine-covered hillside, every log hand-milled, every stone in the foundation carried up from the river.

The river. He could hear it from here, that constant rush of water over rocks. Ram pump clicking away, pushing water up to the cistern. The micro-turbine he'd installed spinning endlessly, feeding power to the battery bank. When the Starlink satellites passed overhead—and they would, in about twenty minutes if he'd calculated right—he'd have enough juice to check on the outside world.

Not that he cared much about the outside world anymore.

Dean pulled the hinge from the forge. The iron had reached that perfect yellow-white glow. He positioned it on the hardy, raised his cross-peen hammer, and delivered three precise strikes. The metal sang. Another fold, another heat, and this hinge would last another decade.

His hands moved with the muscle memory of a thousand repetitions. The work quieted the noise in his head—the screaming, the explosions, the smell of burning hospitals and the sound of little girls who'd never learn to read. Four years since he'd left the service. Four years since he'd traded his rifle for a hammer. 

It still wasn't far enough away.

Strike. Turn. Strike.

The chickens started their morning chorus, clucking and complaining about breakfast being late. Dean smiled despite himself. Spoiled birds. He'd built them a palace—a coop with ventilation, roosting bars at optimal heights, nesting boxes lined with fresh straw every week. They repaid him with eggs and an arrogance that would shame a rooster twice their size.

"Yeah, yeah. I hear you," he called out, his voice rusty from disuse. How long since he'd spoken to another human being? Three weeks? Four? The last time had been a supply run to the tiny trading post in Ovando, population maybe seventy on a good day. Before that, nothing but the crackle of his HAM radio and the artificial cheeriness of video calls he immediately hung up on.

People meant complications. Complications meant pain.

Out here, there was just work. Good, honest work that ended with something useful. Something that wouldn't blow up in your face or get killed because you'd made the wrong call.

Dean quenched the hinge in the water bucket. Steam hissed, and when he pulled it free, the metal had that deep charcoal finish he liked. He set it aside to cool and banked the forge, watching the coals settle into a sullen red glow.

Six-thirty AM. The world was waking up.

He walked to the house, his boots crunching on the gravel path he'd laid last spring. The porch steps were solid—mortise and tenon joints, the old way, the right way. The door swung open on hinges he'd forged two years ago. Everything in this place was built to last, built to weather whatever came.

Inside, the log walls held the night's coolness. Dean moved through the familiar space—kitchen to the left, workshop area to the right, bedroom and bathroom in the back. Minimal. Functional. No clutter, no unnecessary decoration. Just tools, books, and supplies organized with military precision.

He poured water from the cistern tap into a percolator and set it on the woodstove, pushing it over the hottest part where coals still glowed from the morning fire. While it heated, he checked the battery bank status on the charge controller. Ninety-two percent. Good. The turbine had done its job overnight. He flipped the switch for the Starlink terminal and waited for the boot sequence.

Coffee first. Dark roast, ground fresh from beans he'd bought in bulk six months ago. The ritual of it—measure, grind, pour—helped center him. The steam rising from the cup carried memories of a hundred mornings in a hundred places, most of them significantly less peaceful than this.

The terminal beeped. Connected.

Dean settled into the chair at his small desk, coffee in hand, and opened his laptop. The satellite internet was a lifeline he couldn't quite bring himself to cut. Yeah, he wanted isolation, but not stupidity. Knowing what was happening in the world was just good operational awareness. That's what he told himself, anyway.

He pulled up the news sites, running through his usual rotation. Same shit, different day. Political theater in Washington. Tensions with China over Taiwan. Economic reports that meant nothing to a man who traded chickens for coffee beans. 

But then he paused.

The headline was buried on the third site he checked: "Unexplained Power Fluctuations at Nuclear Facilities Across US."

Dean's fingers tightened on the coffee cup. He clicked through.

"…officials downplay concerns… normal variations in grid load… routine maintenance at multiple sites… no cause for alarm…"

His eyes scanned the article with the paranoid precision of a man trained to spot patterns. Seven facilities reporting issues. All within the last forty-eight hours. All with different official explanations that amounted to "trust us."

The back of his neck prickled.

He opened another tab, then another, cross-referencing sources. International news. Energy sector reports. Even a few forums where nuclear engineers hung out and complained about their jobs.

There. Buried in a Reddit thread, a comment from someone claiming to be a contractor at Three Mile Island: "Something's wrong with the SCRAM systems. Multiple redundancies failing simultaneously. This isn't random."

Dean sat back, his coffee forgotten.

Maybe it was nothing. Maybe it was standard grid instability. Maybe it was—

His HAM radio crackled to life, making him jump. The thing was always on, always scanning emergency frequencies. Usually it was just chatter from other operators, weather reports, the occasional prepper convinced the sky was falling.

"—repeat, this is KC7VYY calling any station. Anyone seeing weird readings on your power? My whole setup just spiked. Grid voltage dropped to—"

Static drowned out the rest.

Dean was already moving, crossing to the radio bench against the far wall. His rig was a beast—a modified PRC-117 he'd adapted for civilian frequencies, with enough power to reach halfway across the country if conditions were right. He picked up the microphone.

"KC7VYY, this is KB7DMJ, copy your last. What's your location?"

Silence. Then: "KB7DMJ, I'm in Idaho Falls. Listen, man, I've been doing this twenty years and I've never seen anything like—"

The transmission cut off mid-sentence. Dean waited, watching the radio's display. The signal strength meter had flatlined.

He tried another frequency. "This is KB7DMJ calling CQ on any emergency frequency. Anyone read me?"

Nothing.

He switched to the National Weather Service frequency. Dead air.

Back to the laptop. The Starlink connection had already started degrading. He refreshed. The signal quality meter was dropping in real time—three bars, two bars, one bar, flashing red. The terminal was fighting to maintain a connection, searching for satellites that were either destroyed or blocked by the atmospheric interference spreading across the continent.

The connection died completely.

Dean stood, walked to the window, looked up at the morning sky. Clear. No clouds. No reason for the connection to drop.

Except everything was happening all at once. All the systems dying simultaneously. The power grid, the HAM frequencies, the satellite internet—all of it going dark in a cascade of cascading failures that suggested something far worse than routine maintenance problems.

He grabbed his binoculars from the shelf and stepped outside, scanning the horizon. To the east, beyond the ridgeline, was that… smoke? His gut twisted. That direction was Malmstrom Air Force Base, way out there. One of the biggest nuclear—

No. He was being paranoid. Had to be a wildfire. September in Montana, things got dry.

But wildfires didn't kill your internet. Didn't kill your radio.

Dean lowered the binoculars and stood on his porch, coffee cup still in his other hand, watching the distant smudge of smoke against the morning sky. The chickens had gone quiet. Even the river seemed muted.

Four years. Four years of peace. Of silence. Of waking up without the weight of other people's lives on his shoulders.

The hammer in the forge had gone cold.

He took a sip of coffee. It had gone cold too.

"Probably nothing," he said aloud, his voice swallowed by the vast Montana wilderness.

But Dean Jacobs hadn't survived twenty years in Special Forces by ignoring his instincts. And every instinct he had was screaming that this wasn't nothing.

This was something.

He drained the coffee in one long pull, walked back inside, and started making a list of things he'd need to check. The water filtration system. The ammunition stores he'd buried in the secondary cache last year.

Just in case.

Because if there was one thing the Army had taught him, it was this: when everything goes quiet at once, that's when you need to be the loudest about your preparations.

The last good year was ending.

Dean just didn't know it yet.

---

*End of Chapter 4*
# Chapter 5: Zero Hour

Dean didn't sleep that night.

He sat in his workshop, listening to the radio frequencies cycle through static and silence. The emergency channels that should have been flooded with coordinated response—National Guard, FEMA, all the acronyms that meant someone was managing the crisis—were empty. Or dead. Possibly both.

By midnight, he'd tried every frequency he could access. Nothing. Just the soft hiss of dead air and his own breathing.

The Starlink terminal remained dark on his desk, blipped to life with one satellite connected. It had died hours ago along with everything else—the news page he was viewing refreshed with new satellite imagary that showed the shapes of mushroom clouds across the continental United States. Not one. Dozens. Hundreds. With confirmation that same was occuring all over the globe, China, Russia, the United Kingdom, Australia.

Now there was nothing. No internet. No HAM radio reaching anyone. No news. No word from the outside world at all.

Just him. His property. The Montana wilderness.

And whatever was coming.

He stood, walked to the window. Three AM. The sky should have been clear—this far from any cities, the stars were usually bright enough to read by. But tonight, something was different. A faint luminescence hung in the eastern sky, not quite dawn, not quite aurora. Something in between.

Radiation? No. Too early for that. The fallout wouldn't reach Montana for—

Dean checked his mental map. Wind patterns. Distance from reactor sites. The continental geography he'd studied obsessively over the past four hours. Malmstrom Air Force Base was maybe eighty miles east. If they were hit, the fallout would drift westward on the jet stream. Hours at least before it reached this far.

But the sky was wrong.

He went back inside and spent the rest of the night preparing. Not panicking—panic was a luxury—but being methodical. He filled every water container he had. The cistern would be contaminated eventually, and boiling didn't stop radiation. He opened the freezer in his workshop—solar-powered, kept at minus-ten—and verified his frozen meat stores. Months of food, at least.

The HAM radio stayed on, scanning frequencies. Every hour, nothing but static. Occasionally, fragments of transmission would cut through—a voice shouting something about sheltering in place, then gone. Another voice reading off radiation levels that meant nothing without context. Then silence again.

By dawn, the sky in the east had turned an ugly yellow-gray.

---

The smell came at midday.

Not a normal smell. Something burnt and chemical, underneath it all something metallic that made Dean's teeth ache. He was in the garden, checking on the cold frames where he grew greens year-round, when it hit him. His first instinct was to go inside. His second was to monitor it, to understand it.

He walked to the ridge above his homestead, binoculars in hand, and looked east. The sky had darkened considerably, the sun a dull orange disk behind a haze that was definitely not clouds. The air felt different—heavier, charged with something his body recognized as wrong without his mind being able to fully process it.

Fallout. This had to be it.

Dean descended to the house and made a decision. The safe room. He'd started building one last year, an underground shelter dug into the hillside next to his house. Not paranoia—not exactly. Just the knowledge that governments fall, supply chains break, and sometimes you need to be under the earth.

He'd never finished it completely. Concrete had seemed excessive, expensive. He'd lined it with plastic sheeting and built a sealed hatch door with rubber gaskets. It would help. Might help. Better than nothing.

He began the process of sealing his life away.

Everything not essential got locked down. The animals—he moved the chickens, goats, and pigs into the barn, as sheltered as possible, with feed and water for a week. It was inadequate and he knew it. If the fallout was bad, if it lasted long, the animals would die. But they'd have a chance. More than the animals on every other farm that existed.

He sealed his rainwater collection system—the pipes that fed from the roof to the cistern. Once this began, he couldn't use rainwater for weeks. He'd have to rely on stored water and whatever he could filter.

Inside the workshop, he took inventory with military precision. Canned goods. Dried food. Rice, beans, salt. Medications. The medical kit he'd maintained obsessively for years. Battery-powered LED lanterns and extra batteries. Books. Paper. Matches in waterproof containers.

The safe room was small—eight feet by twelve feet, dug into the hillside, concrete floor and walls of packed earth reinforced with old boards. He'd been planning to improve it for months. Now it would have to be enough.

Dean carried supplies down through the hatch, stacking them methodically. Water—hundreds of gallons, stored in sealed containers. Food, medications, tools. His rifle and ammunition. A radiation detector he'd bought years ago and somehow forgotten he had, buried in a storage locker. Batteries for that too. Hand-crank radio for when the normal one inevitably failed.

By evening, the yellow-gray haze had become a dark brown twilight, even though the sun was still above the horizon. The birds had gone silent. Dean had never realized how much of his day's soundtrack came from birds until they stopped singing.

He was sealing the last of the stored water containers when he noticed the powder.

At first, he thought it was dust from his work. Then he realized it covered everything—the workshop table, the window ledge, the containers themselves. Fine gray powder, almost invisible but definitely there. He touched it with one finger, watched it smudge. Not dust. Something else.

Fallout.

It had arrived.

Dean immediately sealed the workshop and moved everything essential into the safe room. He couldn't risk contamination. Once the radioactive particles were inside his main living space, he'd never fully get them out. Better to abandon the workshop, keep the house itself as a secondary shelter, make the safe room his primary refuge.

He moved fast, efficiently, the tactical part of his brain taking over. Seal the windows. Close off the non-essential rooms. Create a contamination barrier—he rigged up plastic sheeting over the bedroom door, creating an airlock of sorts. Not perfect, but better than nothing.

By full dark—which came much earlier than it should have, with the sky that oppressive brown-gray—Dean was essentially sealed inside his house. The main room became his shelter. Windows covered. Door sealed. The safe room accessible through a careful decontamination process he was inventing as he went.

He'd made a small mistake, he realized. He hadn't filled his bathtub. He did that now, letting the clean water run before it inevitably became contaminated. A full tub was crucial—water for washing, water for drinking, water for everything. Once the system was contaminated, this would be his reserve.

The radiation detector sat on his workbench, silent and dark. He didn't turn it on. He didn't want to know the numbers. Numbers would make it real in a different way. Numbers would make it numbers instead of something he could manage.

---

The second day without the grid was worse than the first.

Dean had always known civilization was a thin veneer over barbarism. He'd lived in countries where that veneer had been stripped away. But knowing it intellectually and living it were different things. The silence outside his sealed house was profound—no cars on distant roads, no planes, no hum of civilization.

Just wind. And whatever was falling from the sky.

He spent the day in the safe room, listening to the hand-crank radio. Battery on his PRC-117 had died sometime in the night, the static finally stopping mid-scan like the universe itself had given up. The hand-crank radio worked, barely. Most stations were off the air. The ones that were broadcasting were confused or panicked or both. One coherent message from FEMA, repeating on loop: "Shelter in place. Do not attempt to leave your residence. Radiation levels are dangerous. Shelter in place."

Shelter in place. As if most people had the supplies he did. As if most people had a sealed room underground and enough food and water to live like a monk for six months.

He sat in the dim light of an LED lantern and tried not to think about the millions of people who didn't.

By the third day, something was wrong with the air pressure in the house. Or with his ears. Or both. The sealed windows felt like they were pushing inward. His sinuses ached. He tried to convince himself it was just stress, then gave up the pretense.

The radiation was accumulating. His sealed house had helped, but it wasn't a perfect seal. Gaps existed. Air had to move. Things leaked. And outside, the sky was falling.

On the morning of the fourth day, everything changed.

Dean was sitting in his main room, trying to ignore the ache in his joints that probably wasn't radiation poisoning but definitely might be, when he noticed something wrong with the light. It wasn't coming from the windows anymore—he'd covered those. It was coming from inside the room, from everywhere and nowhere.

A glow.

Not light. Something else. Something that made his eyes water and his skin itch just looking at it. The air itself seemed to be luminescent, filled with particles that caught light from a source he couldn't identify.

Dean stood, moved to the window, risked looking through a gap in the plastic sheeting.

The sky had changed again. It wasn't just dark now. It was actively dark—the kind of darkness that doesn't come from night or clouds but from something hanging in the air itself, something solid enough to block out the sun. But underneath that darkness was a faint glow. Green. Just barely visible, like foxfire or St. Elmo's fire, but distributed through the entire atmosphere.

It was beautiful. And it was the most terrifying thing he'd ever seen.

Dean pulled back from the window, his heart hammering. That glow meant something. Something fundamentally wrong with the world. Something that the old knowledge he'd accumulated—the survival books, the military training, the three years of careful self-sufficiency—wasn't equipped to handle.

He was still staring at the wall, trying to process, when the hum started.

It wasn't sound, not exactly. It was something felt, something that made his bones resonate and his teeth ache. It came from outside and inside simultaneously, like the world itself had become an instrument and something was playing it.

The green light intensified. The hum deepened. And Dean realized with absolute certainty that something was happening. Something beyond nuclear fallout. Something beyond the scope of human catastrophe.

---

The appearance came at dusk, which had become meaningless—the same brown-gray darkness now, at any time of day.

Dean was in his safe room, rationing water and trying to ignore the persistent ache in his joints and the slight nausea that came and went, when the hum stopped.

The silence that followed was worse than the sound. It was the silence of the world holding its breath.

Then: a voice.

Not through the radio. Not through the HAM equipment or any technology. A voice that seemed to come from the air itself, from inside his head and outside simultaneously, achingly cheerful and utterly alien.

"Ohhhhh my gosh, what is all this? This is such a MESS! Okay, okay, I can fix this. Girl boss energy, let's get this squared away!"

Dean had never heard a voice like it. Southern accent, college-aged inflection, utterly casual, like someone commentating on a minor inconvenience rather than the nuclear destruction of human civilization.

"Nuclear reactions? How quaint! Okay, so we're gonna need to stabilize this. Hold on, I'm deploying countermeasures. Don't worry, humans—zero-gravity nanobots coming your way! Think of them like tiny little robots that will, um, fix the radiation problem. I'm basically a genius!"

Dean pulled himself up from the cot in the safe room, moving toward the hatch. He didn't know why. Some compulsion pulled him toward the surface, toward the source of the voice.

"Now, small thing, you seem particularly... wait. Oh no. Oh no no no, I am SO sorry about that!"

The world lurched.

Dean found himself throwing up, not from nausea but from the sensation of something physical hitting him. Not a sound wave. Not wind. Something solid, something that felt like being hit by a wall made of fire and electricity and every nerve in his body screaming.

When he could see again—when the white-out of pain faded enough for vision to return—something had changed fundamentally.

The safe room was gone. He was lying on the ground, actually on the ground, the earth itself beneath him. The house above him—no, he could see through the earth, could see the house, could see through the house, could see everything at once in a way that shouldn't have been possible.

No. That was the pain talking. That was his nervous system shorting out.

"Oh sweetie, I am SO sorry! I didn't mean to—you're not supposed to be there when I deploy the containment field! Okay, okay, no judgment, I'm going to fix this, you're going to be totally fine!"

The voice was inside his head now, and it sounded like it was smiling.

Something wrapped around him. Not physical, not exactly. Something that felt like being held and burned and crushed and healed all at once. His skin was on fire. Every cell was screaming. His bones felt like they were vibrating at a frequency that would shatter him into component atoms.

But underneath the pain, something else was happening. Something was being done to him, in him, around him. It felt like hands—countless hands—rebuilding him from the inside out. Fixing things that were broken. Replacing things that were damaged.

Rebuilding him specifically.

"There we go! Okay, you're gonna live! Probably! Medical science is kind of not an exact science! Now, I'm going to ask you a very important question, okay? Really important question. What do you want your world to look like?"

Dean couldn't speak. His vocal cords were being remade. His lungs were being rewoven. His heart was being restarted with something that felt like electricity and hate and purpose.

"Hello? Can you hear me? I'm trying to save humanity here! Just give me a vision of what you want and I'll make it happen! Isn't that awesome? I'm basically a wish-granting entity!"

Dean's mouth opened. The words came out slurred, broken, but they came:

"What... what the hell are you?"

The voice laughed—bright, cheerful, utterly without understanding of the weight of what it carried.

"I'm Zero! And sweetie, I'm about to make you paradise! Or at least, I'm going to try! But I really need that vision thing, okay? What do you want your world to look like?"

The pain was fading now, being replaced by something else. Clarity. Wrongness. A sense that something fundamental had changed in his body, in his mind, in the world itself.

Dean lay on the ground—he could see his hand, trembling, in the contaminated earth of his ruined homestead—and thought about the question.

His world? What did he want? Everything was gone. Civilization was falling. Radiation poisoned the air. And some god-like entity was asking him what he wanted.

He laughed. Actually laughed, the sound wrong and bitter coming from a throat that was still learning to work again.

"What, this new hellscape isn't already enough like a survival RPG?" The words came out before he could stop them, sarcasm and desperation mixed together. "Why don't you just make it so I have to get powerful through suffering and there are literal demons or something?"

"Ohhhhh," Zero said, and her voice had changed. It had taken on a note of determination. "That's... actually kind of brilliant? I mean, you might be joking, but that's actually kind of a perfect metaphor for survival and growth and—you know what? Yeah. Yeah, I'm going to do that. That's the vibe! Thank you, sweetie! You just saved the whole human race by describing your own system!"

"Wait, no, I was—"

But Zero was already gone. Not departed, exactly. More like she'd pivoted away, her attention turned to something bigger and broader. The voice in his head changed from singular to plural, from one entity to something that sounded like a vast intelligence thinking through countless channels at once.

"Okay, let's do this thing! Deploying RPG system framework... calibrating to user preferences... oh wow, this is gonna be FUN! Radiation-based experience mechanics? That's dark, human, I like you! Demons from adjacent dimensions? Sure, let's open some rifts! Making people suffer to get stronger? That's... well, that's dark, but okay! Full system integration in three... two... one..."

The world lurched again.

This time, it was different. It wasn't pain. It was like a door opening somewhere in the fabric of reality. Like something being turned on that had always been off. Like the universe itself was remembering something it had forgotten.

And then, in Dean's mind, a simple display materialized:

**STATUS**

**Name:** Dean Jacobs
**Level:** 1
**Experience:** 0%

**Stats:**
Strength: 12
Speed: 10
Reflexes: 11
Recovery: 50
Radiation: 5/5

**Health:** Critical (Healing...)
**Active Effects:** Regeneration (Passive)

The display shimmered, adjusted, became more solid.

"There!" Zero's voice was back, and she sounded genuinely proud. "You've got a system now! Everyone on Earth does! I basically just saved humanity by giving you all a game! You can level up! Become stronger! Fight demons! Isn't that awesome? Okay, gotta go handle the other dimensions now, but like, enjoy your new paradise! Love, Zero! ❤️"

And just like that, she was gone.

The presence that had filled his mind, that had remade his body, that had ended the world—she withdrew, turned her attention elsewhere, disappeared into the vast space between realities.

Dean lay on the ground, his homestead burning around him from internal radiation, his body remade by nanobots, his world ended and restarted by a god-like entity who had misunderstood a sarcastic joke.

A message appeared in his vision, and he read it with numb clarity:

**SYSTEM ANNOUNCEMENT**

**Welcome to the Radiation Event Framework!**

**You are now eligible to gain power through exposure to radiation. Exposure = Experience = Leveling! The stronger you become, the better you can survive in your new world. Warning: Radiation exposure is extremely painful. Advance at your own risk.**

**Congratulations! You have unlocked the first ability: REGENERATION (PASSIVE). You will slowly heal from wounds when not actively wounded.**

**Also, there are demons now. Oops! They're from other dimensions. They're probably going to try to eat you or whatever. Stay alive!**

**Good luck, human! You're going to need it! ❤️ - Zero**

Dean lay in the contaminated dirt of his ruined property, his body still healing from remittance, and let out a laugh that turned into a scream that turned into something in between.

The last good year was over.

The apocalypse had begun.

And somewhere in the sky, in the radiation-filled air, something moved. Something that didn't belong to this world. Something with teeth and hunger and the faint glow of bioluminescence.

The first demon had arrived.
